# Rules for writing a card

A card is one whole French sentence. The learner already knows every part except
one new piece, so they work that piece out from the rest. Never a bare word.
Never a drill.

Only the very first sentence is built up one word at a time —
`Je` → `Je veux` → `Je veux un` → `Je veux un café`. Fragments are allowed there
and nowhere else. After that, every card is a full sentence.

Every card must pass four tests.

## 1. One new thing

Use only words already taught, plus exactly one new element — a word, an ending,
or a small function word (*ne…pas*, *et*, *au*).

- Count against everything taught so far, not just the last card.
- Two things may share one card:
  - a subject and its verb form — *Vous voulez*, *Elle veut*. Neither works alone.
  - the first time a noun needs a new article or preposition, the two arrive
    together — *au marché*. Every later noun reuses the now-known word.
- Never put two new content words on one card (two verbs, or a verb and its object).
- Free, doesn't count as the new thing: an ending once the base word is known; and
  the singular of a regular *-er* verb (*je / tu / il mange* sound the same, so one
  unlocks all three — but *veux / veut* are different sounds and cost a card each).

Only *ne…pas* is new:
`Je mange du pain` → `Je ne mange pas de pain`

## 2. A small step from the last card

Each card should feel like one small move from the card right before it. You must
be able to name the move in a few words: insert a word, swap a word, change the
person, or add to the end. If you can't name it, the step is too big.

Test 1 asks *one new thing vs. everything known*. This asks *how far from the
previous card*. Both must hold.

Insert, swap, change person:
```
Je veux un café              → Je veux boire un café              (insert a verb)
Je veux boire un café        → Je veux boire quelque chose        (swap the object)
Je veux boire quelque chose  → Vous voulez boire quelque chose ?  (change the person)
```

## 3. Repeat early, vary late

Early cards hold the sentence still and change one slot. This keeps the effort near
zero while the basics go in — repetition here is on purpose. Later, once the grammar
is known, change the frame instead, so old grammar gets used in many shapes. (Where
the switch happens: see [plan.md](plan.md).)

Repetition is only good when it earns its place — it sets up the next card, or
drills a new basic. Repetition that just swaps vocabulary in the same frame and
teaches nothing is the thing to avoid: not `J'ai faim` / `J'ai soif` back to back.

Good repetition — a rest that sets up the next card:
`Je mange du pain` (nothing new) → `Je ne mange pas de pain` (new: negation)

## 4. Bring old words back

When a card has an open slot, sometimes fill it with a word taught long ago, not
only a recent one. This keeps old words alive inside new sentences, which teaches
use, not just recognition.

- The app already re-shows whole old cards on its own schedule. This is different:
  it puts an old word into a *new* sentence.
- Test 2 keeps the frame close to the last card; this test chooses what goes in the
  changed slot — so a small step can still carry an old word.
- Reach further as the deck grows: short reaches early, wider reaches late.

## Across the deck

The four tests judge one card. These two judge a band of forty — no single card can
pass or fail them, so check them when the band is done.

### Ask as often as you tell

*Est-ce que* is the workhorse, and it should arrive early. It is a prefix: it turns
any statement already known into a question and changes nothing else, so it costs one
card to learn and is free forever after. Introduce it as soon as there is a *vous* or
*tu* form to attach it to — inside the first twenty-five cards — and make it the
default question from then on.

About a quarter of every band should be a question. Three forms, in this order of
weight:

- ***Est-ce que*** — the default. Neutral, always correct, works with every verb.
- **Intonation** — the statement with a rise: `Vous voulez un café ?` This is what
  people say most; it costs nothing new after the first one, so use it freely.
- **Inversion** — only where real speech still inverts. These are fixed formulas,
  learned whole, not a pattern to generalise:
  `Comment allez-vous ?` · `Quelle heure est-il ?` · `Puis-je …?` ·
  `Pouvez-vous …?` · `Voulez-vous …?` · `Avez-vous …?`
  Never invent a new inversion to drill the pattern — spoken French doesn't use it.

### Keep the persons moving

The card is heard before it is read, and person lives in small sounds: *veux / veut*
are identical, *voulez / voulons* are not; *mange / mangent* collapse, *mangez*
separates. A deck that sits in *je* drills the one form that needs it least.

- No more than about half a band in *je*.
- Every band uses at least four persons, plural included.
- *tu* and *vous* both belong. *tu* is the everyday one between people who know each
  other; *vous* is for strangers, work, and anyone older. Choose per sentence and
  hold it — never mix the two inside one card.
- Rule 4 picks *which* old word fills a slot; this picks *who* the sentence is about.
  Changing person is still a small step (rule 2).

## And always

- **Adult and real — not narrow, not dull.** The default voice is a grown-up's
  everyday life (coffee, work, being tired, money), not a children's book ("the
  happy dog eats bread"). But this is a register, not a filter: never skip a common,
  useful word because it isn't "adult," and keep the subjects moving so the deck
  never goes grey. A word earns its place by being useful and real — adult is just
  the tone.
- **Real** — only what a person would actually say. A connector must mean something:
  "I want to eat but I'm drinking coffee" is a real contrast; "I eat an apple but I
  drink coffee" is not.
- **Known words only** — every word from [vocabulary.md](vocabulary.md), none invented.

## The whole check, in one line

One new thing · one small named step from the last card · repetition that earns its
place · old words pulled back in · real adult French · known words plus one.
