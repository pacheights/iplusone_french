# Sentences

The deck, in order. Each line is one card: the French sentence, the English, and
the single new element the card teaches.

- `[rest]` — teaches no new word. It re-uses known words to drill a form just
  taught or to set up the next card.
- `[rest + note: …]` — no new word either, because the thing being taught *has*
  no word of its own: elision, asking by intonation, or a verb form spelled
  exactly like one already known. The explanation carries it.

## Batch 1 — être

Three cards to open, then all nine subject pronouns against one held-still
adjective, then être spent with the persons mixed.

Adjective agreement (`fatiguée`, `fatigués`) is free — one mechanical rule, so
the explanation carries it and it never costs a card. Verb forms are the
opposite: each is earned separately.

*Est-ce que* carries the questions. Inversion appears only where it is how the
question is actually asked — here, `Où est … ?`.

## Batch 2 — avoir

Same shape: one card to put an object on the table, then the forms against that
held-still object, then avoir spent.

Cheaper than batch 1 — the pronouns, `ne … pas`, and the whole question
apparatus already exist and are reused. Beyond its own forms, avoir adds the
article system (`un / une / des`), the `de` that replaces those articles under
negation, and `il y a`.

Elision (`j'`) is the mechanical rule already taught at cards 19 and 22, so it
rides in the explanation rather than costing a card.

## Batch 4 — pouvoir

The first block that needs no setup card. Pouvoir takes an infinitive, not an
object, and that slot is already open and stocked, so the block starts at full
speed on card one.

Its real job is asking permission — the first block where the lesson is
*register* rather than structure: `Est-ce que je peux entrer ?`,
`Je peux entrer ?` and `Pouvez-vous …?` are the same question in three rooms.
Inversion earns its place here; `Pouvez-vous` is genuinely how the polite
version is asked.

It closes on pouvoir against vouloir: wanting and being able are different
things, and French keeps them apart.

## Batch 5 — devoir

The third verb to take an infinitive, so it needs no setup card either — and the
third whose je and tu forms are spelled alike, which by now is a pattern rather
than a surprise. The rest card says so.

Two things make it harder than the last. `il faut` has no real subject: the il
stands for nobody, exactly as in `il y a`, and falloir turns up in no other
present form, so it is taught whole. And `il ne faut pas` is not the negative it
looks like — it forbids rather than excuses.

`pourquoi` is pulled forward from block 27 so that `parce que` has something to
answer. `je voudrais` enters as a frozen politeness form, on the same condition
`ça va` waits on: the verb underneath is already known.

The block closes on all three modals in one sentence, which is what the last
three were building toward.

## Batch 6 — aller

Two jobs, taught in that order: going somewhere, then going to do something. The
second is the near future, and it costs no word of its own — a conjugated aller
with an infinitive behind it is the whole construction — so it arrives on a rest
card and the explanation carries it.

Before aller can go anywhere there has to be somewhere to go, so `à` and its
contraction `au` enter first, on être. `le travail` and `la maison` are held back
from block 5 for the same reason: both want `à`, and without it they only make
sentences nobody says. Both forms are chosen again on every place the block
names — `au travail`, `à la gare`, `à l'école`, and `en ville`, which refuses
`à la` altogether and is learned whole. The verb itself enters last of the four
setup cards, as an infinitive behind a known `veux`: the name of the verb before
the paradigm that varies it.

`ça va` waits for this block by design: it is the most-said sentence in French,
and it lands as a shortcut rather than an opaque blob once the verb underneath
has been drilled. `Comment allez-vous ?` is its formal opposite, and one of the
few places inversion is genuinely how the question is asked.

It is also where earlier verbs are pulled back at the persons that go quiet
first — `vous êtes`, `ils ont`, `nous avons`, `vous voulez` — and where the
article system is exercised away from the block that taught it.

## Batch 7 — faire

The verb's name comes first, on a card behind the near future block 6 just
finished teaching: `Je vais faire quelque chose`. Then the paradigm, which is
short in one respect and long in another — `faire` has five written forms for
six persons, since `fais` serves both je and tu, but three of the five are
irregular in a way no rule predicts. `faisons` is said *fuh-zon* rather than
*fay-zon*, `faites` refuses the -ez every other verb takes, and `font` sounds
its ending where most verbs swallow it.

`du sport` is what the persons are drilled against. It gives the partitive nine
turns well away from the block that taught it, and its own negative —
`Je ne fais pas de sport` — is where `du` collapsing to `de` gets asked for
rather than merely mentioned. The two activities behind it choose the other
article: `faire la cuisine` and `faire le ménage` take the definite one, because
the cooking and the housework are whole jobs rather than amounts.

The weather adjectives enter plain — `Le café est chaud` — and only then does
`faire` take them over, so the impersonal `il fait` never shares a card with the
first sight of its adjective. `Quel temps fait-il ?` closes that section as a
fixed question, and is the only place `le temps` shows its other meaning.

`qu'est-ce que` and `quoi` are pulled forward from block 24, where the question
words are installed properly. They come here because `faire` is what you ask
about: `Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?` is the question the block exists to make
possible, and `Tu fais quoi ?` is the same question in the register people
actually use with each other.

### The verb, and something to do

## Batch 8 — regular -er verbs

The first block whose lesson is a pattern rather than a verb. `parler` has been
a bare infinitive since card 145, so no setup card is needed: the paradigm opens
on card one, and `vite` is what holds still — nine persons all say it naturally,
and it pulls back an adverb silent since block 5.

Five spellings cover six persons, and the two that collapse are the block's real
content. `je parle` and `il parle` are the same word. `ils parlent` is worse
than that: the -ent is silent *and* both `il` and `ils` reduce to a bare *i*
before a consonant, so `il parle vite` and `ils parlent vite` are the same
sound end to end. Nothing in the audio separates them; the written pronoun is
the only thing that does, and card 278 exists to say so.

Then the pattern is spent rather than restated. `regarder` — also already an
infinitive — pays once and its other five forms ride free, with a card for each
of them anyway, because a form that never appears has not been taught. From
`écouter` on, a verb costs one card and earns three or four: a different person,
a question, a negative (md/rules.md, "The -er endings").

## Batch 9 — more -er verbs, adverbs, frequency

Card 311 is a switch, not a lesson. The -er endings were installed in block 8,
so every -er infinitive already in the deck — `travailler`, `aider`, `chercher`,
`trouver`, `rester`, `entrer` — can be conjugated from here on without paying
for it again. One card turns on all six.

That frees the block's four verb slots for words the deck does not have.
`arriver`, `commencer`, `arrêter` and `oublier` all stand on their own without a
preposition or an indirect object, which is what a verb needs at this stage.
`commencer` costs one extra card: `nous commençons` takes a cedilla, because a
plain c in front of o would be said like a k.

The adverbs are about placement. French puts them straight after the verb where
English puts them in front of one — `je parle toujours vite` is word for word
*I speak always fast*. `ne … jamais` closes the block: `jamais` replaces `pas`
rather than joining it, and it flattens the article behind it exactly as `pas`
does.

## Batch 10 — stem-changing -er verbs

These end in -er and are not in the free class. The stem moves, and one rule
covers five of the six: it changes in the four forms where the ending is silent
— je, tu, il, ils — and stays exactly as the infinitive has it at nous and vous,
where the ending is heard. The sound drives it. When nothing follows, the last
vowel of the stem has to carry the word.

`manger` is the odd one out, and so was `commencer` back in block 9: those two
change only at nous, and only to protect a sound the spelling would otherwise
lose.

Three of these verbs — `manger`, `payer`, `appeler` — have sat in the deck as
infinitives since blocks 3 and 5, unusable because their forms would have been
guessed wrong. This block pays what was owed.

Each verb gets its changed stem, its unchanged stem, and a third use, with the
persons rotated so no one of them carries the block. A third of the cards open
on something that is not a person.
